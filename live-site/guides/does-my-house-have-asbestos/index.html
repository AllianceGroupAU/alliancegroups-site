Does my house have asbestos?

The build year tells you how likely it is. Where it is tells you what to watch out for. Here is how to work out whether your home has asbestos — before anyone drills, cuts or sands.

Start with the build year.

Queensland Government guidance is simple: if your house was built before 1990, there’s a good chance it contains some asbestos.

  • Before 1990 — likely to contain asbestos materials somewhere in the building.
  • 1990 to 2003 — less likely. Asbestos was banned in building products in 1990, but some homes built in the 1990s and early 2000s still used asbestos products until the total ban came into effect in 2003.
  • After 2003 — unlikely in the original build. Older sections, extensions or reused materials can still contain it.

Renovated homes are the tricky ones. A 1970s house with a 2010 kitchen can still have fibro behind the new tiles, under the vinyl or in the eaves.

Eight places to look.

  1. Roof sheeting and gutters — corrugated asbestos cement roofs, capping and old gutters, especially on garages, sheds and carports.
  2. Eaves and soffit sheeting — flat sheets under the roof overhang.
  3. Wall and ceiling sheets (fibro) — internal and external walls, enclosed verandahs, extensions and ceilings.
  4. Meter and fuse boxes — older meter boards were often made with asbestos.
  5. Wet-area splashbacks — behind tiles in bathrooms, laundries and kitchens.
  6. Vinyl flooring and underlay — vinyl tiles, sheet vinyl backing and the sheeting under it.
  7. Fences — flat or corrugated asbestos cement fencing.
  8. Sheds and outbuildings — walls and roofs of sheds, garages and old laundries.

You cannot tell by looking.

Asbestos cement sheeting looks like ordinary fibre cement sheeting. As the Queensland guidance puts it, you can’t tell if a material contains asbestos just by looking at it. If you need to know for certain, a sample has to be analysed by an accredited laboratory.

Until you know, treat suspect material as if it contains asbestos.

What to do.

Leave it alone

Do not cut, drill, sand, scrape, water-blast or break suspect material. Do not sweep, dust or use a household vacuum on broken material — that makes fibres airborne.

  1. Keep it intact. Asbestos cement in good condition, left undisturbed, is far lower risk than broken or weathered material.
  2. Take a photo from a safe distance and note where it is.
  3. Get it checked before any work that would disturb it — renovation, rewiring, plumbing, re-roofing or demolition.
  4. Tell your trades. Anyone working on the house needs to know about suspect materials before they start.

Before you renovate.

Most asbestos exposures in homes happen during renovation. Plan for it up front: identify suspect materials, have them tested or treated as asbestos, and have any asbestos removed correctly before the rest of the work starts. In Queensland, more than 10 m² of bonded asbestos must be removed by a licensed removalist — see the removal rules.

Alliance can remove the asbestos and then carry out the repair and reinstatement, so the renovation is not left waiting between contractors.

Questions

Quick answers.

Still unsure? Call 0403 126 276.

My house was built in 1985. Does it definitely have asbestos? +

Not definitely, but it is likely to contain some asbestos materials. Have suspect materials checked before any work that would disturb them.

Is asbestos dangerous if I leave it alone? +

Bonded asbestos in good condition that is not disturbed is much lower risk. The risk comes when it is broken, weathered, cut, drilled, sanded or water-blasted and fibres are released.

How do I get asbestos tested? +

A sample is taken and analysed by an accredited laboratory. Taking a sample disturbs the material, so it should be done carefully. We can tell you whether testing is worth doing before quoting removal.
